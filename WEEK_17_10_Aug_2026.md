## One-Time Passwords: The Shared Secret

### 🔍 What is it?

When you set up Google Authenticator, Authy, or any TOTP app, the setup QR code is not magic. It is a plain `otpauth://`
URI carrying a secret key, usually rendered in base32, something like `JBSWY3DPEHPK3PXP`. Your phone stores it. The
server keeps its own copy. And that is the entire relationship: from this moment on, the two sides never need to
communicate about codes again.

This is symmetric cryptography in its most approachable form. Both parties hold the same secret, and every future "
authentication" is just both sides independently running the same computation and checking whether the results agree.
There is no code being sent to your phone, no lookup, no callback. The code was always computable by anyone holding the
secret. Typing it in simply proves you are one of those two parties.

### 💡 In simple terms

Two spies are given identical codebooks before parting ways. Years later, one can prove their identity to the other over
a noisy radio channel by reading out the correct phrase for today's page. Nobody transmitted the phrase in advance.
Holding the same book and agreeing on which page to use is all the coordination they will ever need.

### 🌟 Did you know?

The QR scan is the most security-critical moment of the whole scheme. The secret crosses the air exactly once, in
plaintext, as a picture. Anyone who photographs your enrollment screen owns your second factor forever. This is also
why "I'll just screenshot the QR code as a backup" is both a genuinely useful recovery trick and a genuinely dangerous
file to leave in your camera roll.

There is a server-side implication too. Unlike your password, which the server stores as a slow salted hash, the TOTP
secret must be stored in a recoverable form, because the server needs the actual bytes to run the HMAC. A database
breach that leaks password hashes gives attackers work to do. One that leaks TOTP secrets gives them your second factor
as-is. This asymmetry is one of the reasons the industry keeps pushing toward WebAuthn and passkeys, where the server
stores only a public key and there is nothing symmetric to steal.

## HOTP: A Password from a Counter

### 🔍 What is it?

HOTP, defined in RFC 4226, is the ancestor of the code generator in your pocket. It answers a simple question: given a
shared secret, how do two parties produce a short, single-use number that both can compute but nobody else can predict?

The recipe is one line:

```
code = truncate(HMAC-SHA1(secret, counter)) mod 1_000_000
```

The counter is a simple integer that both sides increment on each use. HMAC gives you 20 bytes of output that behave
like random noise to anyone without the secret, and the truncation step boils those 20 bytes down to something a human
can type in the six-digit box. Change the counter by one and the entire HMAC output changes beyond recognition, which is
exactly the point: knowing today's code tells you nothing about tomorrow's.

### 💡 In simple terms

Imagine a tear-off notepad where every page shows a different six-digit number, and both you and the bank hold identical
pads. Each login, you both tear off one page and compare. The pads never need to talk to each other. They just need to
have been printed from the same master and torn in sync.

### 🌟 Did you know?

The truncation step hides a neat trick called dynamic truncation. Rather than always reading the first four bytes of the
HMAC, the algorithm reads the last four bits of the HMAC and uses them as an offset into the HMAC itself, then extracts
four bytes starting there. The output indexes into itself. The RFC's authors did this to avoid any fixed-position bias,
and it costs nothing.

And about SHA-1: yes, it is broken, and no, it does not matter here. The famous SHA-1 attacks produce collisions, two
inputs hashing to the same output. HMAC does not rely on collision resistance, so HMAC-SHA1 remains sound for this use.
RFC 6238 defines SHA-256 and SHA-512 variants anyway, but there has been no fire forcing migration, which is why your
2010-era authenticator setup still works.

The counter design has one practical wrinkle: desync. If you generate a few codes without logging in (pocket presses
happen), your counter runs ahead of the server's. Servers handle this with a look-ahead window, checking the next few
counter values and resynchronizing on a match. Keep that window small, though, because every extra accepted value is
another guess an attacker gets for free.

[RFC 4226: HOTP](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4226)

## TOTP: When the Counter Is the Clock

### 🔍 What is it?

TOTP, RFC 6238, is a one-line modification of HOTP that removed its biggest operational headache. Instead of a stored
counter that can drift out of sync, the counter becomes something both parties already share: the time.

```
counter = floor(unix_time / 30)
```

That is the whole change. Every 30 seconds the division lands in a new bucket, the counter ticks, and the code rotates.
Your phone computes the code from its clock and the secret. The server does the same from its clock and its copy of the
secret. Same inputs, same HMAC, same six digits. No network, no state to persist per login, no counter to resynchronize.
This is why the authenticator app works in airplane mode, on a phone that has been offline for a month.

### 💡 In simple terms

Two lighthouse keepers on opposite cliffs agree to change their lamp color every 30 seconds according to a shared
schedule. A ship's captain who knows the schedule can verify they are looking at the right lighthouse just by checking
the color against their watch. The lighthouses never signal each other. The clock is the coordination.

### 🌟 Did you know?

The clock is also the failure mode. If your phone's clock drifts, your codes are computed in the wrong time bucket and
get rejected. Servers compensate by accepting codes from the adjacent window or two, giving 30 to 90 seconds of
tolerance. Google Authenticator even has a "time correction for codes" setting buried in its menus that syncs against
Google's servers without touching your system clock.

That tolerance is a real security dial, not a freebie. Every extra accepted window is another valid code at any given
moment, which roughly doubles an attacker's odds per guess. This is why server-side rate limiting on code attempts is
not optional hardening but a structural requirement: a six-digit space is only a million values, and a patient attacker
with unlimited tries needs no cryptography at all.

One honest limitation to keep in mind: TOTP proves you hold the secret, not that you are talking to the right website. A
phishing page can ask for your code and relay it to the real site within the 30-second window, and this attack is fully
automated in modern phishing kits. TOTP stops credential-stuffing cold, but real-time relay is exactly the gap that
passkeys and WebAuthn were designed to close, because they bind the authentication to the origin.

[RFC 6238: TOTP](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc6238)

## Zero-Knowledge Proofs: Convincing Without Revealing

### 🔍 What is it?

Every authentication scheme so far in this issue proves knowledge of a secret by revealing something derived from it. A
password reveals itself. A TOTP code reveals six digits computed from the secret. Each is a small leak, bounded and
managed, but a leak. Zero-knowledge proofs ask the maximalist question: can you convince someone that a statement is
true while revealing nothing beyond the fact that it is true? Not a derived value, not a hint, nothing a verifier could
reuse, replay, or learn from.

Surprisingly, yes. A zero-knowledge proof is a protocol with three properties. Completeness: if the statement is true
and the prover is honest, the verifier ends up convinced. Soundness: a lying prover cannot convince an honest verifier,
except with negligible probability. Zero-knowledge: the verifier learns nothing else. That last property has a
beautifully strange formal definition: everything the verifier saw during the proof could have been simulated by someone
who never knew the secret at all. If the transcript could have been faked, the transcript carries no knowledge.

The trick, in most classic constructions, is randomness plus repetition. The prover answers randomized challenges that
someone without the secret could pass only by luck, and each round halves a cheater's odds. Twenty rounds shrink the
bluffing probability to about one in a million. Certainty is never absolute, just driven as close to it as the
verifier's patience demands.

### 💡 In simple terms

The canonical story is the Ali Baba cave. A circular cave has one entrance that forks into two passages, A and B, joined
at the far end by a magic door that opens only with a secret phrase. Peggy claims she knows the phrase. Victor waits
outside while Peggy walks into one passage, chosen by her at random. Victor then walks to the fork and shouts which
passage she must come out of. If Peggy knows the phrase, she can always comply, opening the door if needed. If she does
not, she comes out the right side half the time by luck. After twenty rounds of shouting, Victor is convinced beyond
reasonable doubt. Yet a bystander watching the whole exchange learns nothing: they saw Peggy walk in and come out the
requested side, which is exactly what a lucky faker's transcript would look like. Victor gains certainty; the recording
of the event proves nothing to anyone else.

### 🌟 Did you know?

The field was born in 1985 when Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali, and Charles Rackoff published "The Knowledge Complexity
of Interactive Proof-Systems". The paper had been rejected by conferences three times before landing, then went on to
win the inaugural Gödel Prize in 1993, and Goldwasser and Micali each later received the Turing Award. The Ali Baba cave
story itself comes from a 1990 paper by Jean-Jacques Quisquater and colleagues titled "How to Explain Zero-Knowledge
Protocols to Your Children", which is exactly as charming as it sounds.

Classic zero-knowledge proofs are conversations, which is awkward at internet scale: you cannot have a blockchain
interrogate a prover twenty times. The Fiat-Shamir heuristic fixes this by replacing the verifier's random challenges
with a hash of the prover's own messages, turning the dialogue into a single self-contained artifact anyone can check.
That leap is what made zk-SNARKs practical, the succinct proofs behind privacy-preserving cryptocurrencies like Zcash,
where you can prove a transaction is valid without revealing sender, receiver, or amount. The same machinery now powers
age verification schemes where you prove "over 18" from a passport signature without disclosing your name or birthdate.

The most cinematic application might be nuclear disarmament. In 2016, Princeton researchers demonstrated a
zero-knowledge protocol for warhead inspection: a way for inspectors to verify that an object is a real warhead matching
a declared template, without learning anything about its internal design. The same idea that lets you prove you know a
password without typing it could someday let nations verify treaties without exchanging weapon blueprints.

## Sampling Logs with a Dice Roll

### 🔍 What is it?

Picture a familiar incident: a platform team notices that one busy API service is pushing thousands of log lines per
second into the logging cluster, and almost all of it is the same success-path "lookup completed" line from a client
library. Nobody reads thousands of identical success lines per second. But everybody pays to ship, index, and store
them.

The standard fix is probabilistic sampling: log roughly 1 percent of successful calls, and keep logging 100 percent of
errors. The entire sampling decision is one line:

```typescript
const sampled = Math.random() < successRate;
```

Why does this work? `Math.random()` returns a uniform float in `[0, 1)`, meaning it can return 0, never returns exactly
1, and every value in between is equally likely. For a uniform draw, the probability of landing below a threshold `r` is
exactly `r`. Set `successRate` to `0.01` and the comparison is true for about 1 in 100 calls. No buckets, no state, no
math library.

### 💡 In simple terms

A busy factory produces thousands of identical widgets per hour. Quality control does not inspect every widget. They
pull one at random from every hundred or so off the line. If something systematic goes wrong, it shows up in the random
pulls soon enough, and the inspectors are not buried under widgets that are all fine.

### 🌟 Did you know?

The edge cases work out for free, which is the mark of a well-chosen formulation. `Math.random() < 0` never fires, so a
rate of 0 means "off". `Math.random() < 1` always fires, because the generator never returns 1, so a rate of 1 means "
log everything". No special-casing, no conditionals around the conditional.

Sampling successes while keeping errors at 100 percent is a deliberate asymmetry. Success logs answer "roughly how much
traffic and how fast", which survives a 1-in-100 thinning perfectly well: multiply observed counts by 100 and your rate
estimates are unbiased. Error logs answer "what exactly went wrong at 3 AM", and there is no multiplying your way back
to a stack trace you threw away. Cheap signals get sampled, expensive-to-lose signals do not.

And if the mechanism feels familiar: this is exactly what `DD_TRACE_SAMPLE_RATE=0.01` does inside Datadog's tracer. Same
uniform draw, same comparison against the configured probability, decided once per trace. If you have ever wondered why
trace-derived metrics scale with the sample rate config, this one line is the mechanism behind the mystery.

## Why Random Beats Every-Nth

### 🔍 What is it?

The obvious alternative to a dice roll is a counter: log every 100th call. It sounds cleaner, even thriftier, no
randomness involved. It is worth resisting, for two reasons that generalize well beyond logging.

First, a counter is shared mutable state. A client library in a server process gets hit concurrently from every request
handler, so an every-Nth counter is either racy (increments get lost, the effective rate silently drifts) or
synchronized (you just added contention to your hottest code path to save log lines). A random draw needs no state at
all. Every request decides alone, and concurrency is a non-issue by construction.

Second, and more subtly: deterministic every-Nth sampling can alias with periodic traffic. If some cron fires requests
on a rhythm that happens to interleave with your modulus at the wrong phase, its requests can land on the never-logged
beats every single time. That cron becomes invisible in your logs, not occasionally but permanently, and nothing looks
wrong. Random draws have no phase for the traffic to align with.

### 💡 In simple terms

Film a spinning wagon wheel at 24 frames per second and the wheel can appear frozen or even rolling backwards, because
the camera samples on a fixed beat that resonates with the rotation. Your logs are the film, the cron job is the wheel.
Sample on a fixed beat and a periodic process can hide between your frames forever. Sample at random moments and nothing
periodic can hide.

### 🌟 Did you know?

This is the same aliasing phenomenon that signal processing has been fighting since the 1920s. The Nyquist-Shannon
theorem says a periodic signal sampled below twice its frequency does not just blur, it masquerades as a different
signal entirely. The wagon-wheel effect in old westerns, moire patterns in photos of striped shirts, and the cron job
missing from your logs are all the same mathematics wearing different costumes.

There is a middle path worth knowing about for when you need reproducibility: hash-based sampling. Instead of
`Math.random()`, hash a stable identifier like a trace or correlation id and compare the hash against the threshold. You
keep the no-shared-state property, gain determinism (all services sample the same request the same way, so distributed
traces stay complete instead of getting holes), and still avoid phase alignment because a good hash has no useful
correlation with request timing. This is how trace-id-based sampling works in most tracing systems, and it is the reason
a sampled distributed trace has all of its spans rather than 1 percent of them.

## Aho-Corasick: A Thousand Needles, One Pass

### 🔍 What is it?

Plenty of systems face the same awkward search problem: one piece of text, hundreds or thousands of keywords to find in
it. A spam filter checking a message against a blocklist of phrases. A moderation pipeline scanning comments for flagged
terms. A log processor watching for any of five hundred known error signatures. The naive approach is a loop: for each
pattern, scan the text. With `m` patterns over a text of length `n`, that is `m` full passes, and it gets slower with
every entry you add to the list.

The proper tool is the Aho-Corasick algorithm, invented in 1975 by Alfred Aho and Margaret Corasick at Bell Labs. It
compiles the entire dictionary of patterns into a single automaton: a trie of all the patterns, augmented with "failure
links" that say "if matching fails here, this is the longest partial match you are still inside". Then the text is
scanned exactly once, character by character, walking the automaton. Every pattern occurrence is reported along the way.

The scan cost is O(n + number of matches), independent of how many patterns are in the dictionary. Ten patterns or ten
thousand: one pass, same speed.

### 💡 In simple terms

Suppose you are proofreading a document for a list of two hundred banned phrases. The naive way is to read the document
two hundred times, once per phrase. The Aho-Corasick way is what an experienced editor actually does: internalize the
whole list first, then read the document once, noticing every banned phrase as it streams past. The preparation happens
once; the reading never repeats.

### 🌟 Did you know?

Alfred Aho is the A in AWK, and also the Aho of the Dragon Book, the compiler textbook that taught generations of
engineers. Margaret Corasick was an information scientist at Bell Labs, and the 1975 paper was one of the earliest
demonstrations that a bibliographic search system could be sped up by an order of magnitude with the right automaton.

The failure links are the actual genius of the construction. They generalize the KMP string-matching idea from one
pattern to a whole dictionary: when a match breaks, you do not restart from scratch, you slide to the longest suffix of
what you have seen that is still a prefix of some pattern. All that sliding is precomputed at build time, so at scan
time every character advances the automaton in constant amortized time.

You have been using it without knowing. `grep -F` with multiple fixed strings historically used Aho-Corasick (the trivia
bonus: fgrep's original implementation was written by Aho himself). Intrusion detection systems like Snort use it to
check packets against thousands of attack signatures. Virus scanners like ClamAV use variants of it to match malware
signatures. Spam filters, profanity filters, DLP scanners: anywhere the question is "which of these many known strings
appear in this text", this automaton is usually underneath.

## BM25: The Ranking Formula That Refuses to Retire

### 🔍 What is it?

BM25 stands for Best Match 25, a ranking function developed in the 1990s by Stephen Robertson and Karen Sparck Jones out
of the Okapi information retrieval system. Thirty years later it is the default relevance score in Elasticsearch and
Lucene, powers Solr, ranks results in academic databases like PubMed, and does the keyword half of retrieval in most RAG
pipelines feeding LLMs today.

It earned that longevity by fixing two real flaws in its predecessor, TF-IDF. First, TF-IDF rewards repetition without
limit: a page mentioning "coffee" 500 times outranks one mentioning it 5 times, long past the point where extra mentions
signal anything. BM25 adds term saturation, so the score from repeated occurrences climbs quickly at first and then
flattens. Second, TF-IDF quietly favors long documents, which have more room for term occurrences. BM25 normalizes
against the average document length in the collection, so a focused 500-word post can beat a rambling 10,000-word
article.

The knobs are two parameters: `k1` (typically 1.2 to 2.0) controls how fast repetition saturates, and `b` (typically
0.75) controls how strongly length normalization applies. The rare-word weighting comes from IDF, same instinct as
TF-IDF: matching "kubernetes" tells you more than matching "the".

### 💡 In simple terms

Term saturation is seasoning. The first pinch of salt transforms the dish, the second pinch helps, the tenth pinch
changes nothing except your blood pressure. TF-IDF scores the dish by total salt added. BM25 scores it the way a person
tastes it: early mentions matter a lot, and the curve flattens from there.

### 🌟 Did you know?

The name is wonderfully honest about how research actually works. It is called Best Match 25 because it was, roughly,
the 25th weighting scheme the Okapi team tried. BM11 and BM15 existed and were worse. Science is iteration with version
numbers.

A tiny worked example from the article that inspired this section: with the query "cat dog" over three short documents,
a document mentioning only "cat" scores 0.47, one mentioning only "dog" scores 0.47, and one mentioning both scores
0.94. Covering more distinct query terms beats repeating one term, which matches human intuition about relevance and is
exactly the behavior the saturation term buys you.

And despite the semantic-search era, BM25 is not being replaced so much as being paired up. Vector search finds
documents that are conceptually similar but can whiff on exact identifiers, product codes, error strings, or function
names. BM25 nails exact terms, needs zero training data, runs fast over millions of documents, and its scores can be
explained term by term when someone asks why a result ranked first. Modern retrieval stacks increasingly run both and
merge the results, a pattern called hybrid search. The 30-year-old formula did not survive by resisting the new thing.
It survived by being the half the new thing cannot do.

## 🎯 That's a Wrap!

Every topic this week is a small piece of math carrying a production-sized load, and each one earns its keep by making
some hard coordination problem simply not exist.

**One-Time Passwords** started with the shared secret: one QR scan, two copies of a key, and no further communication
ever needed. The enrollment moment is the crown jewels, and the server-side storage requirement is why passkeys are
coming for this scheme's job.

**HOTP** showed the core recipe: HMAC over a counter, truncated to six digits. The self-indexing truncation trick, the
irrelevance of SHA-1's collision troubles, and the look-ahead window for desync are all hiding inside a two-line
formula.

**TOTP** replaced the counter with the clock, and the whole synchronization problem evaporated. The 30-second buckets,
the drift-tolerance windows, and the rate-limiting requirement are one trade-off surface: every second of forgiveness is
also a wider guessing target.

**Zero-Knowledge Proofs** took the idea to its logical extreme: convince a verifier that a statement is true while
revealing nothing they could reuse or replay. Randomized challenges halve a cheater's odds each round, the Fiat-Shamir
trick turns the conversation into a single verifiable artifact, and the applications run from private transactions to
nuclear treaty inspections.

**Sampling Logs with a Dice Roll** turned thousands of log lines per second into a trickle with `Math.random() < rate`,
uniform distribution doing all the work, and the 0 and 1 edge cases behaving correctly for free. Errors still log at 100
percent, because you cannot multiply a discarded stack trace back into existence.

**Why Random Beats Every-Nth** explained the two failure modes the dice roll avoids: a shared counter is either racy or
a contention point, and deterministic sampling can alias with periodic traffic so thoroughly that a cron job hides from
your logs forever. The wagon-wheel effect, but for observability.

**Aho-Corasick** compiles a whole dictionary of patterns into one automaton and finds every occurrence in a single pass,
so a thousand-entry blocklist costs the same scan as a ten-entry one. Same machinery inside fgrep, Snort, and ClamAV
since before most of us were writing code.

**BM25** closed with the search formula that outlived its own era: term saturation, length normalization, two tunable
knobs, and a name that admits it took 25 tries. Semantic search did not kill it; hybrid search made it half of the
answer.
